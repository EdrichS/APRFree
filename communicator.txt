APRFree: connecting to banks and other apps
===========================================

Written October 6, 2026. Plans, thoughts and options for making the app do more of the typing for
the user. Nothing in this file is built yet.

Facts about outside services (what they offer, who may use them, what the rules are) were checked
on the date above and change often. Each one is marked with its source at the end. Confirm any of
them again before building on it. Nothing here is legal advice.


1. THE STARTING POINT
---------------------
APRFree has no network code. Everything is typed in by hand and stays on the iPhone. That is the
promise in the privacy policy the user accepts during setup, and it is why the App Store privacy
answer can be "Data Not Collected".

So the first question for any connection is not "can it be done" but "what does it cost that
promise". This file sorts every option by that:

  Tier A   Nothing leaves the iPhone. The promise holds. No server.
  Tier B   Account data comes from a data provider. Needs a server and changes the promise.
  Tier C   The app moves money. Needs all of Tier B and a great deal more.


2. WHAT A CONNECTION COULD DO FOR THE USER
------------------------------------------
These are the jobs, in the order they would save the most effort:

  a. Fill in a debt when it is added: balance, APR, credit limit, minimum, due day.
  b. Keep balances right. Today a card's balance only moves when the user records something or does
     the monthly statement check-in. This is the app's biggest accuracy gap.
  c. Record payments by itself, instead of "pay in the bank's app, then mark it paid here".
  d. Bring in what the statement says: statement balance, minimum due, due date, interest charged.
  e. Get the user to the right place to pay, quickly.
  f. Actually make the payment.


3. TIER A: NOTHING LEAVES THE IPHONE
------------------------------------

A1. Open the bank's app from a payment line (job e)
    Each line of a split, and each debt's "Pay" button, gets an "Open Chase" button that jumps to
    the bank's app, or its website if the app is not installed.
    - How: universal links where the bank supports them, otherwise its URL scheme, otherwise the web
      address. Checking whether an app is installed needs each scheme listed in the Info.plist.
    - Effort: small. A table of links per brand, plus a place for the user to add their own.
    - Risk: links are not published by the banks and can change. Needs a fallback every time.
    - Already on the future list. A good first step.

A2. "Mark paid" from the reminder itself (job c, partly)
    A due-date reminder gets two buttons: Mark Paid and Open Bank. The payment is recorded without
    opening APRFree. The widget could get the same button now that it is interactive.
    - How: notification actions, and an App Intent like the one behind the widget's eye button.
    - Effort: small to medium.

A3. Import a statement file (jobs b and d)
    The user downloads a statement or an activity export from their bank and opens it with APRFree
    from Files or the share sheet. The app reads it on the iPhone and fills in the monthly check-in.
    - Formats worth supporting, easiest first: CSV, OFX/QFX (the old "Quicken" download most banks
      still offer), then PDF.
    - PDF statements differ by bank, so this means one small reader per bank: statement balance,
      minimum payment due, payment due date, interest charged. Text comes out with PDFKit.
    - Effort: medium, and ongoing, because banks redesign statements.
    - This is the best fit for the app as it is: the check-in screen already exists, and this
      turns it from typing into confirming.

A4. Scan a screenshot or the screen of the bank's app (jobs a, b and d)
    The user shares a screenshot of their card's page, or points the camera at a paper statement.
    Text recognition on the iPhone (the Vision framework) finds the balance, the minimum and the due
    date, and the user confirms them.
    - Effort: medium. Recognition is reliable; deciding which number is which takes the work.
    - Works for every bank and for Klarna and Affirm, which offer no data access at all (see 6).

A5. Shortcuts, Siri and App Intents (jobs c and e)
    Expose "Record a payment", "Add a charge", "What is due next" and "Where should $X go" as App
    Intents. They then work from Siri, Spotlight, the Action button and Shortcuts.
    - Worth checking: Shortcuts has an automation that runs when a card in Wallet is used with Apple
      Pay and passes along the amount. If it does what it appears to, a user could set it to add
      each Apple Pay purchase to the right card in APRFree as a charge, with no server involved.
      Not verified. Try it before promising it.
    - Effort: small per intent.

A6. Calendar (job e)
    Offer to put due dates and paydays in the user's calendar, so they show up where the user
    already looks. Needs calendar permission and the EventKit framework.
    - Effort: small.

A7. Apple's FinanceKit: Apple Card, Apple Cash and Savings (jobs a, b, c and d for those accounts)
    Apple's own way for a finance app to read balances and transactions from the accounts in Wallet.
    The data is read on the iPhone, with the user's permission, so no server is needed and the
    promise holds.
    - Who may use it: Apple grants it per app. The app must be a finance app distributed on the
      App Store in the United States or the United Kingdom, and the request has to come from the
      Account Holder of an ORGANIZATION developer account. Individual accounts are not eligible.
      This matters: an account in your own name cannot get it.
    - What it covers: in the US, Apple Card, Apple Cash and Savings with Apple, on iOS 17.4 or
      later. In the UK it works through open banking, on iOS 18.4 or later.
    - So in the US it helps only people who have an Apple Card. For them it would be complete:
      balance, credit limit and every transaction, with payments recorded automatically.
    - Effort: medium, after approval.

A8. iCloud sync (not a bank connection, but the same kind of help)
    Keep the data in the user's own private iCloud database so a second device, or a new iPhone,
    has it. The developer cannot read a private database.
    - It does mean the data leaves the iPhone, to Apple, so the privacy policy would need to say so
      and it should be a switch that is off until the user turns it on.
    - How this is described in the App Store privacy answers should be checked against Apple's
      definitions at the time.


4. TIER B: ACCOUNT DATA FROM A PROVIDER
---------------------------------------
This is what people usually mean by "connect my bank". The user signs in to their bank through a
provider's screen, and the provider hands the app the account's data.

What changes the moment this is added:
  - A server is required. Every provider gives the developer a secret key that must never be inside
    the app, and long-lived access tokens for each connected account that have to be stored safely.
  - The user's financial data passes through, or is reachable by, that server and the provider.
    "Nothing leaves your iPhone" stops being true for anyone who connects.
  - The App Store privacy answers change from "Data Not Collected" to financial information being
    collected and linked to the user. The privacy policy, the in-app policy text and both privacy
    manifests change with it.
  - Providers review an app before giving production access, and expect security practices to match.
  - It costs money for every connected account, every month. Get a quote; do not guess.

B1. Plaid, Liabilities product (jobs a, b and d; payments seen, not made)
    The most widely used provider in the US, and the one whose data lines up best with APRFree.
    For a credit card it returns the APRs (purchase, cash advance, balance transfer, with the
    balance each applies to), the minimum payment for the next cycle, the next payment due date, the
    last statement balance and its date, the last payment and its date, and the credit limit. It
    also covers student loans and mortgages.
    How it maps onto the app as built:
      last statement balance + date   ->  the monthly statement check-in, done automatically
      minimum payment amount          ->  the debt's minimum (today: fixed until the user edits it)
      next payment due date           ->  the due day, and a check on the app's own due-date rule
      purchase APR                    ->  the debt's APR, kept current if it is variable
      credit limit                    ->  utilization, without the user entering limits
      last payment amount + date      ->  a payment entry, so "mark paid" is confirmed, not typed
    Gaps: auto loans and personal loans are covered unevenly, and pay-later plans not at all.

B2. Others of the same kind
    MX, Finicity (Mastercard Open Finance), Yodlee, Akoya (owned by banks, and built on the FDX
    standard), Teller, and Stripe Financial Connections. Coverage, price and data detail differ.
    For this app the deciding question is which one returns credit card statement data (minimum,
    due date, APR), not just balances and transactions.

B3. Providers built around debts: Method and Spinwheel (jobs a to d, and f)
    Both start from the person rather than the bank login. The user confirms who they are, the
    provider finds their debts through a credit-bureau lookup (a soft pull, which does not affect a
    credit score), and then reads current balances, due dates, rates and payment amounts from the
    lenders it supports. Both can also send payments to those debts.
    - Appeal: the user does not sign in bank by bank, and one connection finds every card and loan.
    - Cost: this collects identity information (name, phone, date of birth), which is a much bigger
      step for an app whose whole pitch is that it knows nothing about you.

B4. What the US rules say right now
    A federal rule (CFPB, "Section 1033") was meant to give consumers the right to share their
    bank data with apps of their choosing. It was finalised in October 2024, then blocked by a
    court, and the agency is rewriting it; a revised proposal went for White House review in August
    2026 and had not been published when this was written. Until something is in force, access to
    bank data in the US runs on private agreements between banks and providers. In practice: use a
    provider, and expect coverage to vary bank by bank.

A sensible shape for Tier B, if it is ever built:
  - A separate "Connected" mode that is off by default. Everything in the app keeps working with it
    off, and the default promise stays true for everyone who never turns it on.
  - The smallest server that can do the job: it swaps the sign-in result for an access token, keeps
    that token, and passes data to the user's own iPhone when asked. It keeps no copy of balances
    or transactions.
  - Bank data is treated like a statement check-in: it arrives as entries the user can see and
    undo, not as silent edits. The undo journal and the entry history already support that.
  - Where the bank and the user disagree, show both and let the user choose once per debt.


5. TIER C: MOVING MONEY
-----------------------
Paying the card from inside APRFree (job f). Possible through Method or Spinwheel, or through a
bank-transfer provider.

  - It turns a planner into something that handles people's money. Mistakes stop being wrong
    numbers and become wrong payments.
  - App Review's guideline for apps that manage money says they should come from the financial
    institution providing the service. A payment feature would be reviewed in that light.
  - Identity checks, fraud, failed and returned payments, disputes and customer support all become
    the developer's problem, whatever the provider handles.
  - Recommendation: not for this app until it has real users, real revenue and a lawyer. A1 and A2
    get most of the benefit: one tap to the bank, one tap to mark it paid.


6. KLARNA, AFFIRM AND OTHER PAY-LATER PLANS
-------------------------------------------
Their public APIs are for shops taking payments, not for a customer's own plan. There is no
supported way for a third-party app to read a person's installments, and the bank-data providers
do not cover them either. For these, Tier A is all there is: a link to their app (A1), a
screenshot scan (A4), or typing it in.

Their own rules, confirmed while checking how APRFree handles installments:
  - Klarna: paying part of a plan early does not change the amount of the next installment, unless
    less than one installment is left, in which case only what is left is taken.
  - Affirm: paying more than is due goes to the balance and does not count toward the next payment.
    But once the month's payment has been made in full, a further payment in the same period counts
    toward the following month. (This is from Affirm's Canadian help pages, the ones the search
    turned up. Check the US wording too.)
APRFree already works this way: one payment settles one installment and any excess comes off the
end of the plan, with no nine-day waiting period. Nothing needed changing.


7. A SUGGESTED ORDER
--------------------
  Step 1  (no server, promise intact)
          A1 open the bank's app, A2 mark paid from the reminder, A5 App Intents.
          Small, safe, and each one is useful on its own.
  Step 2  (no server, promise intact)
          A3 statement import, starting with CSV and OFX, then PDF for the banks you use yourself.
          A4 screenshot scan. Together these make the monthly check-in close to automatic.
  Step 3  (decide first)
          A7 FinanceKit, if you form an organization developer account. A8 iCloud sync as an
          opt-in. Both are Apple-only and need no server of your own.
  Step 4  (a different product)
          B1 as an opt-in Connected mode. Only after Steps 1 and 2 show whether people still want
          it, because it costs money every month and changes what the app is.
  Not planned
          Tier C.


8. QUESTIONS TO SETTLE BEFORE ANY OF IT
---------------------------------------
  - Is "everything stays on your iPhone" the product, or a starting point? Steps 1 and 2 keep it.
    Step 4 ends it for anyone who connects.
  - Would you run and pay for a server? Tier B cannot exist without one.
  - Individual or organization developer account? It decides whether FinanceKit is open to you.
  - Free or paid? A provider's monthly cost per connected account needs something to cover it.
  - Which banks first? Statement import (A3) is built one bank at a time, so start with your own.
